Locked — confirm your name above to begin.
  • Two islands of finches, one with small seeds and one with large, and birds that fly between them. See which females gain by being choosy about a partner's beak, and which lose by it.
  • How choosy, the arrow on the diagram, sets how often a choosy female turns down a male whose beak differs from hers.
  • Drag ◐ on Survival by beak to set how well a bird with one small-beak and one large-beak allele does. Migration sets how many birds swap islands each generation.

Beak, choosiness, matingThe beak gene: a small-beak and a large-beak allele. Small beaks do best on small seeds, large on large.The choosy gene: a female with one or two choosy alleles turns a male down, with the chance on the arrow, when his beak differs from hers (half that when they differ by one allele). She meets up to 4 males; if she turns all 4 down she does not breed.Grey arrows: fixed.

Beak, song, matingA young bird hears a song: its father's, or a male's picked at random on its island. A son sings it; a daughter prefers it. Now and then a bird hears it wrong (1 in 50).The beak shapes the song: with the chance on its arrow, a male sings the song his beak suits instead (a small beak a fast trill, a large beak a slow one, a bird in between either). Large beaks open and close slowly (Podos 2001).How choosy: the chance a female turns down a male whose song differs from the one she prefers. Grey arrows: fixed.
