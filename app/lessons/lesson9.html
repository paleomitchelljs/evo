A

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

Mendel was a physicist who made a pea garden because his abbot told him to. He found consistent ratios. He did back-cross experiments. He realised that important patterns can only be explained by one underlying model. The model he built, very elegantly, out of flower-colour ratios in pea plants, figures out meiosis: you start with two copies of each gene, end with one in each gamete, and there is a randomization step between them.

He had no idea what a nucleus was. He didn't know what mitosis was, or meiosis. He'd never encountered the word chromosome. He didn't know there was a molecule in each cell that drove inheritance. When you learn his rules now, you smuggle in everything you know about cells and chromosomes, which can actively mislead you.

The spot marked on the long chromosome is not dominant, and it is not recessive. They are just big A and little a. Everyone wants to make everything dominant and recessive. A lot of the time there is no dominant and there is no recessive. They are just different.

  • One cell. Two long chromosomes, two short ones.
  • Four spots on every chromosome. One is labelled: big A on one long copy, little a on the other.
  • Replicate, then divide twice — two cells, then four gametes.
  • One drag takes a whole side of a cell: a long chromosome and a short one.
  • Which copy lines up on which side of the plate is drawn fresh every round.
  • The square in step 3 crosses every gamete with every gamete.

Do your cells know what a gene is? No. As nice as it is to split things off into alleles and loci, what actually matters is the physical reality of the DNA strand.

Two dictionaries, different versions, same words. The words are the spots. The slightly different definitions are the alleles.

  • The same cell. Every spot now carries a label.
  • Long chromosome: A B C D on one copy, a b c d on the other.
  • Short chromosome: E F G H on one copy, e f g h on the other.
  • One meiosis gives you two kinds of gamete, and they are opposites.
  • Run it again for the other two. The plate does not line up the same way twice.
  • The same three steps as Stage A.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Recombination is taking a dictionary, choosing a page at random, and ripping it. Then taking a different dictionary, ripping out the same page, and gluing the two together. The closer two spots are, the more likely they stay on the same fragment after a single rip.

It costs you. You are scrambling your genome with somebody else's. Your kid is not as closely related to you as they would be if you reproduced asexually. In the trade-off between inherit and invent, you are reducing the inherit for your own kids.

  • The same cell, the same labels.
  • Every round cuts both chromosomes: one mark on the long one, one on the short.
  • The slider sets how many go on each. Three gaps a chromosome, so one to three.
  • Everything past a mark swaps between the two chromatids it sits between.
  • Where the marks go decides which spots still travel together.
  • The same three steps again.

A locus is just a chunk of DNA selection hasn't recombined apart yet. Functionally speaking, recombination is mutation — super-mutation, at random places, but not necessarily breaking. Selection sees stretches of DNA, not single alleles.

D

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

Stage A told you big A and little a are just different. Now three of the spots do something you can see.

Watch what does not change. The genotypes are one in four, two in four, one in four. Same as Stage A. Same as every stage since. What changes is what the flower does with them.

Big A frills the petal and one copy is enough, so Aa looks exactly like AA. Three flowers in four come out frilled. Two genotypes, one look.

Big B and little b both get used. BB is red, bb is white, and Bb lays down both pigments in stripes. Nothing hides. You read the genotype straight off the petal.

The leaf spot blends instead. EE dark, ee pale, Ee somewhere in the middle. Three looks again, one to two to one again. But the heterozygote is a mix, not both.

Dominance isn't a property of the gene. It's a property of what you're looking at.

  • The same cell. Three spots labelled: A and B on the long chromosome, E on the short.
  • A/a is petal shape, B/b is petal colour, E/e is leaf colour.
  • No marks this time. A and B have nothing cutting between them, so they arrive together.
  • E is on the other chromosome, so it assorts on its own.
  • The chart under step 3 draws the three genotypes as bars, and brackets the looks over them.
  • A bracket over two bars is what dominance is.
  • The same three steps.

E

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.

Height isn't one gene. There are something like 1,700 individual loci associated with height, almost all of them very small effect. The largest single one we have ever found is associated with a 0.07-centimetre difference. Height is a thousand little things, and you add them up.

Here you get eight. Every spot moves size, and the two alleles at a spot move it by different amounts. Some of them add and some of them take away. Green adds. Red takes away. Nothing masks anything and nothing blends — you add the eight numbers up.

Your job is the biggest offspring you can build. Two gametes make an offspring, so start with the biggest gamete.

Look at the two chromosomes you were dealt. Neither one is all green. Neither one is all red. The gamete you want does not exist in this cell yet, and the only thing that can build it is recombination.

Here is the catch. The slider spends the same number of marks on the long chromosome and the short one, and the two of them do not want the same number. One of them does better with two. The other does better with one. You cannot give them both what they want.

So it is a choice, and it is not the obvious one. More cuts is not more size. Set the marks, read what that cell could make, then move them and read it again.

Get the largest gamete you can actually build, and the smallest. Then we will look at what that did to the offspring.

  • The same cell, every spot labelled, and the marks are back.
  • Every spot has a number for each allele. Green boxes add to size, red boxes take away.
  • Nothing is dominant here and nothing blends. The two copies add.
  • The gamete you want carries all eight green alleles. Whether this cell can build it is another question.
  • The slider spends the same number of marks on both chromosomes, and the two chromosomes do not want the same number.
  • Once the marks are all placed, the bench says what the best and worst gamete available at that setting are. Move a mark and it changes.
  • Finding the right setting is only half of it — which long chromatid ends up with which short one is drawn fresh every round.
  • In the histogram the outline is what the grid predicts and the filled bar is your own offspring.

The gamete with all eight green alleles is worth +10.7 and nothing in this cell can build it: the long chromosome needs two cuts to give up B, the short one needs one cut to keep g and h, and the slider only does one number. The best that exists is +10.2, and getting it means taking one cut and letting the long chromosome keep a — which costs 0.5, less than the 0.9 that two cuts costs the short one. That is the whole of it. Recombination does not change the average, it widens what selection has to pick from, and what it can widen is set by where the chromosome will break.
