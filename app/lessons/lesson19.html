This lesson is going to take all of the population genetics processes we have learned, and look at how they can produce differences between populations. We will have drift, mutation, selection, and importantly, migration. The key number to look at here is FST. This is our F statistic: the observed relative to expected heterozygotes, compared not within one individual, but between individuals within a subpopulation. FST is the level of inbreeding, F, in the subpopulation, S, relative to the inbreeding of the total population, T. You can think of this very directly as how different the 2 subpopulations are from one another. An FST of 1 means the 2 populations are perfectly different: you can tell readily which population an individual came from by its genes. An FST of 0 means that the populations are perfectly the same: you cannot tell genetically which population it came from.

We're going to use, as our example here, fish in ponds. Imagine 2 ponds; we'll call them orange and blue. There are fish in each pond. Fish can only move from one pond to the other pond when there's a flood. We're going to control how often a flood occurs, generations between floods, and how many fish move during each flood, the migration during flood. We will also control the number of fish in the pond, the mutation rate, which is going to be the same across ponds, and the recombination rate. For this first part, those are the only toggles we're going to have. Your goal is to reach the target FST using these toggles.

We're sticking with ponds and fish, and we're still monitoring FST. We're now going to base these fish a little more biologically on the stickleback. The stickleback is a little fish, and it can have multiple different forms. Some of them have pharyngeal teeth, teeth in their throat. The extent of these pharyngeal teeth is controlled by a locus called Bmp6. Having these pharyngeal teeth makes them very good at eating prey on the bottom, large prey, things like snails. There's another locus that controls their gill rakers. These are fine structures in their throat that help them filter food out of the water, like plankton. These genes are at separate loci. These are 2 different genes that increase the fish's ability to eat food.

      
Bmp6 causes pharyngeal teeth. Pharyngeal teeth cause you to eat snails. The gill-raker gene causes gill rakers. Gill rakers like plankton, and what we adjust is the number of plankton compared to the number of snails in the 2 populations.

      
There is a fitness surface shown, which shows the fitness of the Bmp6 and gill-raker locus genotypes as a little pair of boxes. They show the position of the fish at the beginning, and the color of the boxes shows you the relative fitness as a result of that. Your goal here is to, again, generate differences between the populations. Except this time, you're going to generate differences at specific loci. So what you're controlling here again is migration rate during a flood, the frequency of floods, the recombination rate, and now fitness.

The individual loci we looked at above in part B were not the only loci. Many other loci involve mutations. Sometimes a mutation can arise in one population, and a different mutation can arise in a different population. Isolated, these two mutations, each arisen in a different population, are fine. They have no fitness effect. But, due to epistasis, if I combine the new mutation from population orange with the new mutation from population blue, they may have an incompatibility: something that causes fish that have both of these new alleles to have lower fitness. We call that an incompatible pair.

      
For this, we're again going to control the same general patterns we did before, with mutation rate being particularly important, and the chance that a pair of mutations is incompatible. Using these dials, we're going to run and see what it does to things like hybrid survival rate and FST between the ponds.

    We're going to focus in only on Bmp6 again, and we're adding a new gene, this throat gene. This throat gene controls the color of the throat of a fish, the color of the skin. There will also be a gene for a preference. So there is the food allele, which controls survival in the 2 populations; a preference allele, which controls how strongly fish tend to mate with individuals of a particular color: AA prefers to mate with black-throated fish, BB prefers to mate with red-throated fish, and AB is non-selective; and then a throat color gene that controls the color of the throat. The location of the throat gene and the preference gene is the important bit, because that's what can be scrambled by recombination.
