Pine trees — do they want their seeds to be eaten? No. The trees that made pinecone seeds that were easy to eat — what happened to them? They died. The trees that survived are the ones that had pinecone seeds that weren't easy to eat. That's more or less all selection is doing — the ones that aren't good enough to make more of themselves don't make more of themselves.

  • Individuals, one locus, two alleles. Each individual is two halves, one per copy.
  • The bars: how many offspring each genotype leaves, against the yellow homozygote. s moves the purple homozygote; h moves the heterozygote, from below both to above both.
  • F is held where you set it, every generation.
  • Bottom plot: how common purple is. A run has to pass through every red window.
  • Tick practice for a run that does not count.

  • Four alleles at one locus, each starting in a quarter of the copies. Each has its own h and s.
  • The grid: every genotype's fitness. A homozygote is 1 + s; a heterozygote adds each allele's h × s.
  • Go runs 200 populations until one allele is left, or 600 generations. Each line is the allele that won, in its colour; dots mark when.
  • The bars: how often each allele won. Match the red outlines.
  • All neutral sets every s to 0.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.
  • 50 populations. Every individual starts on the low peak: no + copies at either of two loci.
  • The trait is how many + copies an individual carries, 0 to 4. The high peak needs all four; anything between sits in the valley.
  • New + copies arise by mutation, 1 copy in 1000 each generation. Selection pushes every one of them back down.
  • The balls are the populations. One has reached the high peak when its average is 3.5. Each run lasts 1500 generations.
  • Tick practice for a run that does not count.
