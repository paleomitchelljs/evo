Alleles code for proteins. What that protein does produces the phenotype. If a protein makes red pigment, you're adding red paint to a flower. A broken pigment allele adds nothing. Add nothing to nothing, you get nothing — white flower.

I have a field with hummingbirds and bees. Hummingbirds like red flowers, bees like white. More hummingbirds than bees, so red has higher relative fitness. Which one likes pink?

  • 36 flowers, 12 of each genotype. The two squares under a flower are its two copies, red or white.
  • red from one copy: how red a flower with one red copy is, from 0 (white) to 1 (as red as two copies).
  • Hummingbirds take flowers at least as red as their edge; bees, flowers at most as red as theirs. Colours they do not take get a few visits. Drag pink or either edge on the middle plot.
  • Go plays a season: each visitor calls on every flower it takes about 8 times, one seed a visit.
  • The bars: seeds per flower, against white. s is how far red sits above white; h is where pink sits on the ruler from white (0) to red (1).
  • Bottom plot: every season you play, pink's colour against its h.
  • Play some seasons first; Start the targets when you are ready. Tick practice for a run that does not count.

Pine trees — do they want their seeds to be eaten? No. The trees that made pinecone seeds that were easy to eat — what happened to them? They died. The trees that survived are the ones that had pinecone seeds that weren't easy to eat. That's more or less all selection is doing — the ones that aren't good enough to make more of themselves don't make more of themselves.

  • Individuals, one locus, two alleles. Each individual is two halves, one per copy.
  • The bars, as in Stage A: how many offspring each genotype leaves, against the yellow homozygote. s moves the purple homozygote; h moves the heterozygote: at 0 it sits with yellow, at 1 with purple, outside 0 to 1 beyond both.
  • The line runs through every individual's offspring against its purple copies. Its slope × the spread of copies is how much offspring and purple go together; ÷ average offspring, it is how far purple moves next generation.
  • F is held where you set it, every generation.
  • Bottom plot: how common purple is. A run has to pass through every red window.
  • Tick practice for a run that does not count.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Recessive is not some extra property. Recessive is the word we use when something doesn't matter as a heterozygote. Things often don't matter as a heterozygote because they're broken, but there's a backup. You have about 100 mutations; almost all of them are either neutral, or they break something.

Two individuals with the same broken locus can produce homozygous-recessive offspring with an embryo-lethal mutation. The more inbred you are, the higher the rate.

  • New mutations arise every generation, at a fixed rate. Each is a new allele at its own locus, and each is harmful: its s is below 0.
  • You set where new mutations' h and s come from: an average and a spread for each.
  • An individual's fitness multiplies over the alleles it carries: 1 + h s for one copy, 1 + s for two.
  • Top plot: grey is every mutation that arose. Dots are the alleles still here, sized by their copies: dark arose in the first 200 generations, pale in the last 200. The edges count copies.
  • Middle plot: the harmful copies an individual carries, and how many selection takes out each generation.
  • Right: every individual, its harmful copies against its offspring. The line's slope × the spread of copies is what selection takes out.
  • Inbreeding test, after the run: offspring of one parent selfed, against offspring of two.
  • Try some runs first; Start the targets when you are ready. Tick practice for a run that does not count.

It's going to be a function of the mutation rate. Full stop. Selection is not in there. It went away. How does selection go away? It's a magic trick. The worse a mutation is, the rarer it is. The less bad it is, the more common. They cancel each other out. Stage D is open.

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.
  • 50 populations. Every individual starts on the low peak: no + copies at either of two loci.
  • The trait is how many + copies an individual carries, 0 to 4. The high peak needs all four; anything between sits in the valley.
  • New + copies arise by mutation, 1 copy in 1000 each generation. Selection pushes every one of them back down.
  • The balls are the populations. One has reached the high peak when its average is 3.5. Each run lasts 1500 generations.
  • Tick practice for a run that does not count.
