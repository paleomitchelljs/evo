A

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

Before any of that: be one of the birds.

It is one year. There are seeds on the ground — soft ones anybody can eat, and hard ones you can only open if your beak is deep enough. What you get to eat is what you have to spend, and it goes on two things: staying alive, and eggs.

A deeper beak opens more of the island to you. It also costs something to grow and to carry, every year, whether it opens anything or not. Find out when that trade is worth making.

  • Two real years off the record you are about to model. One where the seeds went hard, one where they went soft.
  • The soft seeds are split among every bird on the island. The hard ones are split only among the birds that can crack them.
  • Your year = the chicks you leave plus your own chance of getting through it. Nothing else is being counted.
  • The beak charges you twice — once out of the eggs, once out of your own skin — and it charges the same either way whatever kind of year it is.

B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

The way people talk about evolution, usually, is that it's like a force — like gravity. This is wrong. It's more like motion. Evolution is the net change that results from many forces, a lot of things operating at once causing a change.

Here is an island, and twenty generations of it. It rained, seeds grew, birds ate them, and the birds that were left had chicks. Nothing else happened.

You are going to build that one piece at a time. Start with the smallest model there is: birds are born at some rate, and they die at some rate. Get the number of finches right.

  • Forty-five generations in all. You are shown the first twenty.
  • Four pieces. Each one you finish puts a new box on the diagram, a new arrow, and a new slider.
  • The first three are scored on how many finches there were. Nothing in them touches the beak, so there is nothing else to score.
  • Every slider you have already met stays live. A new piece usually means the old numbers were wrong.
  • Rainfall is measured and given to you. It is the only thing on the island nothing else causes.

d <- read.csv("data/clean/island.csv")mostChicks <- 1.0leastDying <- 0.30leftOverEachYear <- 100softFromRain     <- 1.0beakWorth <- 0.00inherited <- 0.50for (t in 1:20) {  # the larder: what nobody ate last year is still there  hard <- 0.86*hard + leftOverEachYear  # and this year's crop, which only the rain makes  soft <- 0.35*soft + softFromRain*d$rain[t]  # with no seeds, nobody is ever short of food  food <- (soft + hard)/n  food <- soft/n + hard/n   # still shared out evenly: no beak in this line  born <- mostChicks  died <- leastDying  born <- pmin(mostChicks, mostChicks*food/(0.62+food))  died <- leastDying + (1.10-leastDying)/(1+food/0.53)  born <- born + beakWorth*(beak-9)  died <- died - beakWorth*(beak-9)  # the parents are done; next year is the chicks  n    <- born * (n - died*n)  beak <- mean(beak) + inherited*(midparent - mean(beak)) + noise}

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Your model sits on twenty generations of record. Now ask it the only question that matters: what happens next?

Ten more generations are already on the island. You have not seen them. Fit on the twenty, freeze what you found, and run it forward into the ten.

"Motion occurred" is a terrible explanation for a traffic accident. A beak that simply pays is the same kind of answer — it tells you the beaks got deeper because deeper was better, and it has nothing to say about a generation it has not been shown.

  • Same four numbers, fitted the same way on generations 1–20.
  • Running it forward is the commitment. You do not get to adjust a model after seeing what it had to predict, so the run happens once.
  • The second arrow appears once you have run the first one forward.
  • Then refit, and lock in when you are ready. A lock-in that misses tells you by how much and hands the sliders back.
  • Bars: 0.20 mm across 1–20 and 0.20 mm across 21–30, both at once.

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

Fifteen more generations, and a piece of news. In generation 31 a different bird reached the island: a couple of hundred pairs of them, much bigger, with beaks that go through a hard seed like it isn't there.

Nothing else changed. The rain over those fifteen generations is ordinary — no drought worth the name, no flood.

Run your model, the good one, forward into them. Then put the newcomer in it.

  • Generations 31–45. The newcomers hold their numbers throughout — they neither build up nor die back.

E

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.

Nothing on this island was pushing in a direction. Rain fell, seeds grew, birds ate what they could reach, and some of them had chicks.

The beaks got deeper for twenty generations and then went back. A bird that arrived in generation 31 turned them round without touching a single one of your numbers.

"Evolution occurred" is a terrible explanation for an organism's trait. Read the table: the models are not separated by how many numbers they carry.

  • All three were fitted on the same twenty generations.
  • Green marks the smallest miss in each column.
  • Nothing in any of them is called selection, and nothing is called drift.
