A

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

Before any of that: be one of the birds.

Here is this year's flock, and here is what the seeds are doing. Some years the soft ones run out early and all that is left is what a hard-seed beak can crack; other years the soft crop holds and a deep beak is just extra weight to carry. You do not get told which kind of year it is — only what it does to a bird built one way or another.

Pick a beak. Follow it through the winter. It has chicks, or it doesn't — and if it doesn't, that line is over and you start again. If it does, you do not get to pick a fresh beak next year: you pick which of its own chicks to be, and go again with whatever it handed you.

  • The flock is shown as it stands this year — you do not set it, you read it.
  • The two panels below it show what a bird built at each beak depth can expect: how many chicks, and what chance of seeing spring.
  • Year one only: slide to a beak depth and lock in. A real chick count comes back — not an average, an actual roll.
  • After that you are choosing among your own bird's actual chicks — their beaks land near yours, not wherever you would like.
  • Only chicks that themselves live to spring count, or carry the line onward — read the survival panel, not just the chick count, or a good year on paper can still end you.
  • The flock keeps changing underneath you, year to year, whether you do well or not. Five years, then your total surviving chicks are checked against a bar.

B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

The way people talk about evolution, usually, is that it's like a force — like gravity. This is wrong. It's more like motion. Evolution is the net change that results from many forces, a lot of things operating at once causing a change.

Here is an island, and twenty generations of it. It rained, seeds grew, birds ate them, and the birds that were left had chicks. Nothing else happened.

You are going to build that one piece at a time. Start with the smallest model there is: birds are born at some rate, and they die at some rate. Get the number of finches right.

  • Forty-five generations in all. You are shown the first twenty.
  • Four pieces. Each one you finish puts a new box on the diagram, a new arrow, and a new slider.
  • The first three are scored on how many finches there were. Nothing in them touches the beak, so there is nothing else to score.
  • Every slider you have already met stays live. A new piece usually means the old numbers were wrong.
  • Rainfall is measured and given to you. It is the only thing on the island nothing else causes.

d <- read.csv("data/clean/island.csv")mostChicks <- 1.0bestSurvival <- 0.70leastDying   <- 1 - bestSurvivalleftOverEachYear <- 100softFromRain     <- 1.0beakWorth <- 0.00inherited <- 0.50for (t in 1:20) {  # the larder: what nobody ate last year is still there  hard <- 0.86*hard + leftOverEachYear  # and this year's crop, which only the rain makes  soft <- 0.35*soft + softFromRain*d$rain[t]  # with no seeds, nobody is ever short of food  food <- (soft + hard)/n  food <- soft/n + hard/n   # still shared out evenly: no beak in this line  born <- mostChicks  died <- leastDying  born <- pmin(mostChicks, mostChicks*food/(0.62+food))  died <- leastDying + (1.10-leastDying)/(1+food/0.53)  born <- born + beakWorth*(beak-9)  died <- died - beakWorth*(beak-9)  # the parents are done; next year is the chicks  n    <- born * (n - died*n)  beak <- mean(beak) + inherited*(midparent - mean(beak)) + noise}

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Your model sits on twenty generations of record. Now ask it the only question that matters: what happens next?

Ten more generations are already on the island. You have not seen them. Fit on the twenty, lock in what you found, and only then find out what it had to say about the ten.

"Motion occurred" is a terrible explanation for a traffic accident. A beak that simply pays is the same kind of answer — it tells you the beaks got deeper because deeper was better, and it has nothing to say about a generation it has not been shown.

  • Same four numbers, fitted the same way on generations 1–20 — those numbers stay live on screen the whole time you're fitting.
  • Watch your model's own line reach into 21–30 as you fit. The actual record there, and how far off you were, both stay hidden until you lock in.
  • Locking in is the commitment: you do not get to adjust this model once you've seen what it had to predict.
  • The second arrow appears once you've locked in, starting at a default value — see what just adding it does before you touch its own slider.

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

Fifteen more generations, and a piece of news. In generation 31 a different bird reached the island: a couple of hundred pairs of them, much bigger, with beaks that go through a hard seed like it isn't there.

Nothing else changed. The rain over those fifteen generations is ordinary — no drought worth the name, no flood.

Run your model, the good one, forward into them. Then put the newcomer in it.

  • Generations 31–45. The newcomers hold their numbers throughout — they neither build up nor die back.
  • They eat hard seeds and nothing else. Whatever they take is gone before your finches get there.
  • Your four fitted numbers do not change, and nothing here is fit: the newcomer's share of the hard seed is given, not searched for.
  • Your model's own line reaches into 31–45 from the start. Run it forward blind and the record reveals itself too — read the mismatch before you fix it.
