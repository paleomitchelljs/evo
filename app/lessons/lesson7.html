For this part, you are going to choose a bird, every year, out of the birds that are actually there.

Three charts are shown below, all scaled on the x axis showing the beak depth. The top row shows the other birds on the island, and what beak depth each of them has. Do that ten times, and if your tally is high enough you're through to Part B. Otherwise you play again.

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

For this part, we're going to combine what we did before — birth-death modelling — with what we just played with in our experiment in Part A, where you follow the chick lineage.

Here we're going to use some data modelled on the Grants' study of finches in the Galápagos, and you're going to first get the birth-death model with a single rate as close as you can to the observed number of finches. It will not be particularly close.

Then, if you look at the flow chart model down at the bottom, you will see that we expand it. What we'll do is, we will have the amount of seeds that are left behind at the end of a year. That's something you can adjust. As you adjust this, try and get your projected model line as close to the empirical data as you can. Remember to read the error, the typical miss, shown beneath each graph as you go.

Once you've successfully managed to do that, you'll have the option to incorporate beak depth into this, such that a bird's survival and reproduction — its birth and death rates — are going to be a function of the environment: how much rain falls to make soft seeds, how many hard seeds last over a year, as well as intrinsic features linked to the bird's traits: how well a deeper beak works on hard seeds, and how heritable a deeper beak is. That is, how much do offspring resemble their parents?

Birds prefer the soft seeds. So in a good year with lots of soft seeds, big beaks just take a lot of energy to grow and provide no benefit. But in a drought when the only seeds available are the uneaten leftovers of previous years…the big beaked birds are the only ones that can eat.

So far, we have fit models to different kinds of data, and the explanation from Lessons 1 to 6 has always been that the point is to predict new, unknown observations.

What you're going to do here is take your model from Part B, which is fit to your data, and project it forward in time to years you have not seen. D

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

So far, we fit the model to observed data, projected forward, and then observed more data to test it. However, in the second exercise the underlying conditions remained the same.

Circumstances can also change.

For this period, we're going to look at the last fifteen or so generations on this island of the recorded information from the Grants. What they saw is that partway through their study, a new species of bird arrived on the island. That new species had a distinct beak shape, and its arrival didn't change the number of seeds and didn't change the way plants grew — but it did change the value of having a beak of a particular shape, by changing the structure of competition.

So we're again going to run our model, projected forward, this time knowing that there was a massive change with the addition of a newcomer. See how our projections erred, and adjust our model to account for this.

  • Generations 31–45. Newcomer numbers hold steady — no growth, no decline.
  • They eat only hard seeds — a standing flock of them, taking their fill off the larder before your finches get there.
  • Nothing refit here — how many newcomers there are is given, not searched for.
  • Commit to a forecast first — click generations 30 and 45 before anything runs.
  • Then run forward blind. You see your model's projection; the record stays hidden.
  • Draw the arrow and watch the same line move — by half, or a great deal more if your fit was close.
  • Forecast a second time with the newcomer in. That is what reveals the record.
