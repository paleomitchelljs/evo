A

Evolution is the study of the change in the frequency of transmissible traits over time. As such it requires a unit, which can transmit traits, and a population — a group of those units. Because in order to be a frequency, it has to be what proportion of the units in there are transmitting or not transmitting those traits. That is an abstract way of saying that here the unit will usually be an organism, a transmissible trait will be an allele, and what we are concerned with are populations: groups of organisms transmitting their traits over time.

Whether those traits influence their own transmission is an important distinction. If a trait influences its own transmission, we say it is selected — it can increase or decrease the probability that it is transmitted, which is what the direction of selection means. But an influence of the trait on whether it is transmitted is not necessary. Any time there is unequal transmission, any time some traits are transmitted more or less often for any reason, the frequency will change. And the smaller a population is, the more dramatically those fluctuations will vary.

For this activity the trait has no impact at all on whether it is transmitted. We are only looking at how populations drift from wherever they began to wherever they end up.

Anything that increases differential reproduction makes evolution go faster. It makes things change more over time. Regardless of whether it covaries with traits. B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

In part A we watched one population drift, over and over again, from different starting points. We can also watch several populations drift at the same time, from the same starting point, and see how far apart they get.

A core feature of drift is that each generation inherits the random noise of the generation before it. That is what makes the frequency move in a way that looks, for any one population, like a trend — you could see it in part A, where an allele would climb or fall as though some force were driving it somewhere on purpose. Nothing was.

The switch below decides whether that noise is inherited. On one setting the parents are drawn from the generation before, so the error accumulates. On the other, every generation is drawn from the frequency the run started at, so the same amount of chance happens every generation and none of it carries forward. That second setting is, roughly, what migration does, and migration comes in a few weeks.

So the first thing is: the random error causes changes — but the random error itself is inherited. Because the random error we're referring to is random sampling error in the heritable variation. All the random effects in a generation get summed together at the next round of reproduction. That's how you get the effect we just saw, where the purple ones emerged triumphant and rose up out of nowhere. They increased through nothing other than the compounding of sampling error being inherited generation after generation. Stage C is open.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Peter Buri took a great many fruit flies and divided them into 107 bottles, each one holding a self-sustaining population. He then watched the frequency of one allele change over time in every one of those 107 bottles. Flies have short generation times, which made this feasible. He controlled how many flies made it into each new bottle. And because the gene showed up as a visible characteristic, he could track it in every bottle — before anybody could sequence anything.

Drift destroys variation. What happened in parts A and B is that you watched, over and over, different populations becoming more different from one another — because different alleles were going extinct. If an allele randomly goes to zero it is gone forever. And alleles will randomly go to zero, because in a pure drift scenario there is nothing keeping them around. With nothing maintaining that variation, the system inevitably collapses to one allele, no matter how many you begin with.

The time it takes to collapse is, in a pure drift scenario, a function of only two things: the starting frequency of the allele, and the size of the population.

You are going to simulate that experiment: 107 bottles, all the same size, all starting at the same frequency.

We have what are called absorbing boundaries. Once I hit zero, the frequency of A is zero in perpetuity. That's just what happens. So I don't need as much sampling error as you'd expect to get big changes. Even if the sampling error is really small, if I let the thing run long enough, eventually I'm going to hit one or zero. And once I do, that variation is gone. Which is why the presence of heterozygosity means you haven't drifted everything to extinction. A heterozygote's existence says: this locus has not fallen yet. We still have variation here. We can still change frequencies. Stage D is open.

You have an absorbing boundary at zero, which matters a lot in biological things. And if you're dealing with fluctuations where there's an asymmetric value to low versus high, the downs matter a lot more, because the downs are the limiting step.

Anything that makes some individuals reproduce more than others makes evolution faster. It makes things change more over time. Regardless of whether it covaries with traits.

Humans like patterns. So when you think of "random," you want it to be: a 1, then a 6, then a 2, then a 5, then a 3, then a 4. That's not how it works. It's very streaky.

And for pretty much every population we look at, the effective population size is smaller than the census. So drift is always stronger than it seems. It is not the average that does it. A herd that sat at a thousand for forty winters and one that averaged a thousand by crashing to fifty for six of them are not the same herd to a gene, and the one that crashed is worth a fraction of its own average.
