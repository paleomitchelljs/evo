A

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

You inherit loci. Do you inherit genotypes? You inherit an allele from each parent — you don't inherit a genotype from either parent. Do you inherit phenotypes? If you have a one-eyed parent, do you get one eye? No. What you inherit from the one-eyed parent are a bunch of individual loci.

What we are building here is a baseline. Our expectation of no change. It is not real. It is not a thing that exists. It requires an infinite number of individuals alive at one time, and it requires teleportation. Do either of those exist in the world? But it is our baseline for comparison.

  • One locus. Two alleles in the pool, A and a.
  • Every individual is two draws out of that pool, one from each parent.
  • The square is every way those two draws can come out. Across: the allele from one parent. Up: the allele from the other.
  • The slider sets how much of the pool is A.
  • The switch moves the lit corner from two A's to two a's.
  • 2,000 individuals are dealt underneath, so the corner can be checked against a count.
  • Lock in each answer. The page counts how many tries it took.

Recessive is not some extra property. Recessive is the word we use when something doesn't matter as a heterozygote. Things often don't matter as a heterozygote because they're broken, but there's a backup.

Dominant and recessive are not a real thing that describes a feature of genes. They are an outcome of the context in which a gene exists. They are always terms about phenotypes, not genes.

In an incomplete dominance system, I can't tell from a purple flower whether it's heterozygous red-blue or homozygous purple. Same phenotype, two possible genotypes. In codominance, both alleles are visible separately — like blood type AB. Type AB tells you with certainty: heterozygous A and B. No ambiguity.

We're analogizing evolution to motion. Newton's first law: an object at rest tends to stay at rest unless acted on by an outside force. This is our object at rest. A gene frequency tends to stay at that frequency unless acted on by an outside force.

One thing that's nice is that alleles are heritable. Full stop. We do not have to question heritability for alleles. Variation, differential replication, heritability of that variation — in any system at all where you have those three, you get evolution.

  • The population from Stage B, handed forward 60 generations.

  • Three more sliders: how many offspring each genotype has.
  • Top plot: the share of the pool that is A, generation by generation. Bottom plot: the three genotype shares.
  • The alleles are inherited and the individuals differ. Both of those are true at every setting on this page.
