A

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

A population evolves, not an individual. If seven of ten breeders die at random, that's evolution, not selection. If three leave the pond, that's also evolution, not selection. Only if all the pale ones die does that become selection. All three change the frequency of variants. Only one is selection.

It's not just the number of individuals that matters. It's anything that increases the variance in mating. Not the actual randomness itself — the variance in it. If everyone has on average two kids, and most have two, that's fine. If most have zero and a few have ten, the population shrinks effectively even if the average is identical.

In the big population, one lightning strike had a 0.005% effect. The smaller population has a 0.5% effect. On its own, that one lightning strike is not going to change much. But it's not literally zero effect. And that one lightning strike is not the only random thing that's going to happen to my population, whether it's 10,000 or 100.

  • One pond. Every dot is a breeder. One gene, two alleles — pale and dark. A breeder carries two copies, so it is pale, mixed, or dark.
  • Every breeder gets a share of the next generation. The shares always average two offspring apiece and the headcount never changes.
  • The spread slider changes how unevenly those shares get handed out. It changes nothing else — not the average, not the headcount, not the alleles.
  • Nothing on this screen knows which allele a breeder is carrying.
  • The dots are the pond. The line underneath is the frequency of the dark allele, generation by generation. Watch them together.

B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

Randomly, the dark ones are a little bit higher one generation — because of a lightning strike. The next generation, the dark ones are also likely to be higher again, because the next generation inherits the random weirdness from the one before it. Then it adds its own random weirdness. And the third generation inherits both of the prior generations' random weirdness. That lightning strike is going to have impacts many generations down the line.

Because each generation doesn't start fresh. You don't start with a clean slate — you start with what you inherited from the previous generation, and what you inherit from the previous generation is biased by all these random events. Lightning strikes. One individual had four kids instead of three. Somebody's parent had triplets. Having triplets is a random event — you don't plan for triplets, they just sort of happen. If somebody has triplets, they make up a bigger share of the genetics in the next generation than if they'd had one kid.

Humans like patterns. So when you think of "random," you want it to be: a 1, then a 6, then a 2, then a 5, then a 3, then a 4. That's not how it works. It's very streaky — you'll get three or four 6s in a row.

  • The amount of randomness per generation is identical under both settings. Only the starting point differs.

So the first thing is: the random error causes changes — but the random error itself is inherited. Because the random error we're referring to is random sampling error in the heritable variation. All the random effects in a generation get summed together at the next round of reproduction. That's how you get the effect we just saw, where the dark ones emerged triumphant and rose up out of nowhere. They increased through nothing other than the compounding of sampling error being inherited generation after generation. Stage C is open.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

We know the frequency of an allele is going to change over time. And if it is neutral then we know it's not going to be selected for. So how is it going to change? Is it going to consistently go up, consistently go down? It'll probably be random. And what happens if it randomly goes to zero? Can it come back from that, or is it gone forever? It's gone.

It's not that there's no reason for it to disappear. There is nothing keeping it around, because it is not being selected for. There's nothing holding it around. There's nothing maintaining that variation.

Only two variables: the size of the system, and the frequency of one allele in it. That's what tells us the time until something goes extinct. Bigger population makes it take longer. Your frequency being higher doesn't mean you're going to win — it just means you've got a better shot than the alleles at lower frequency.

We have what are called absorbing boundaries. Once I hit zero, the frequency of A is zero in perpetuity. That's just what happens. So I don't need as much sampling error as you'd expect to get big changes. Even if the sampling error is really small, if I let the thing run long enough, eventually I'm going to hit one or zero. And once I do, that variation is gone. Which is why the presence of heterozygosity means you haven't drifted everything to extinction. A heterozygote's existence says: this locus has not fallen yet. We still have variation here. We can still change frequencies. Stage D is open.

D

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

Anything that makes some individuals reproduce more than others makes evolution faster. Having a really lopsided sex ratio causes individuals in the rarer sex to reproduce more. There are 100 males, or 100 females, and the population is still going to be a thousand. They're going to have ten kids each. Whereas the 900 of the other sex are going to have a tenth of a kid each. Not quite — you do the math.

The greater prairie chicken does this thing called a lek — all the males gather, pop out throat sacs, do funny dances. Females watch and choose. One year, one male was just way better. Literally every female prairie chicken mated with that one male. All the other males didn't mate that year. Even though half the population was male and half female, every living prairie chicken alive today traces back to this one male.

You have an absorbing boundary at zero, which matters a lot in biological things. And if you're dealing with fluctuations where there's an asymmetric value to low versus high, the downs matter a lot more, because the downs are the limiting step.

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.

Random chance produces patterns more than people expect. That's why you've got to understand the math or run the simulations a lot — to build an intuition for what it looks like. Because the big thing about population genetics is looking at change and asking: how would you know if that change over time is due to random variation or due to selection or due to something else? Is it easy and obvious from the data alone? Or could this data kind of be either one?

Streakiness begs for an explanation in our brains, and you have to resist that tendency. Streakiness does not always beg for an explanation. We invented statistics to say: this streak — this requires an explanation. This streak — this does not.

Selection is the correlation. Drift is the scatter around it. There's the deterministic part — the amount to which the trait covaries with fitness, with differential reproduction. And there's the random error. Because no matter how much your trait covaries with fitness, there's going to be random stuff where things get struck by lightning. You might have a gene that makes your heart great. That doesn't help you if a tiger ate you. In the real world, in finite populations, you can never make something actually perfect. And how far from perfect you are isn't unpredictable — it's a function of your population size.
