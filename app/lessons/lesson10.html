In the big population, one lightning strike had a 0.005% effect. The smaller population has a 0.5% effect. On its own, that one lightning strike is not going to change much. But it's not literally zero effect. And that one lightning strike is not the only random thing that's going to happen to my population, whether it's 10,000 or 100. All the random effects in a generation get summed together at the next round of reproduction. Each generation doesn't start fresh.

A population evolves, not an individual. If seven of ten breeders die at random, that's evolution, not selection. If three leave the pond, that's also evolution, not selection. Only if all the pale ones die does that become selection. All three change the frequency of variants. Only one is selection.

Only two variables: the size of the system, and the frequency of one allele in it. Bigger population makes it take longer. Your frequency being higher doesn't mean you're going to win — it just means you've got a better shot than the alleles at lower frequency.

You are literally zero genetically related to most of your ancestors. You get zero genes from a majority of them. Three generations back, you're usually still related to most. By eight generations back, someone has dropped out of your actual lineage. By around twelve generations back on average, you are genetically connected to fewer of your ancestors than the ones you're still connected to. They passed on zero of their genes that are still inside you.

You're not equally related to your grandparents. And you are not, on a genetic level, even at all related to some of your great-great-great-grandparents. You have no DNA in common with them. None of their DNA made it through to you. Maybe it did to your sister, or your cousin — they didn't just disappear from the family. But for any one individual, there's a set of ancestors in the past from whom you didn't actually inherit any DNA.

Go 32 generations back — about 930 years, year 1100 AD — and you have more ancestors than there are base pairs in your DNA. About four billion. There are about eight billion people alive today. Were there more people alive in the year 1100? No. Something is wrong with this picture. The answer: we're all massively inbred. The genealogy doesn't bifurcate cleanly. The same individuals show up in your family tree multiple times.

We have a massive, humongous amount of inbreeding in every lineage, because everything that sexually reproduces has this. There is no such thing as an unrelated person. The question is not, are they related — the question is to what extent.

In the general population, your chance of finding someone with the same constellation of broken alleles is roughly one over the mutation rate — very, very low. With a close relative, it's a lot higher, because you have a lot more in common that's identical-by-descent.

The greater prairie chicken does this thing called a lek — all the males gather, pop out throat sacs, do funny dances. Females watch and choose. One year, one male was just way better. Literally every female prairie chicken mated with that one male. All the other males didn't mate that year. Even though half the population was male and half female, every living prairie chicken alive today traces back to this one male. Because he danced really well that one year.

It's not just the number of individuals that matters. It's anything that increases the variance in mating. Not the actual randomness itself — the variance in it. If everyone has on average two kids, and most have two, that's fine. If most have zero and a few have ten, the population shrinks effectively even if the average is identical.

Salamanders are not lizards. They're not particularly close to lizards. People assume amphibians, like reptiles, like heat. Even with no biology, you know lizards sun themselves, snakes sun themselves, they get warm, you see them in summer. Salamanders are not that. They breathe through their skin — heat dries them out. They like it cool. Very close to, but not quite, freezing. Late February is the peak of salamander season around here. About 40 degrees and rainy — that is perfect salamander time.

Strong genetic differences between areas are not evidence of local adaptation. It would be easy to assume that as a frequency increases across populations, the trait must be more fit there. But frequency is not fitness. Fitness can affect frequency, but frequency can also be affected by drift, mutation, migration.

So allele frequencies in the new population shift just by random sampling error — no selection involved. Don't assume adaptation when you can explain it with drift. Stage F is open.

As you change scope, the individual becomes the population, and the population becomes the individual. I can shift my scope for the unit of selection. I could talk about cells as units of selection. I could talk about people as a whole. I could talk about families. Are his left and right arms the same individual? It depends on the scope. There's no privileged level. The thing we usually call "the individual" is just the level we happen to be looking at.

An individual who starts out AB starts as a single cell, and they grow. They end up as a gonad full of haploid cells. About half of those haploid cells will be A and half will be B. Did they reproduce the same? No. The haploid cells are reproducing differentially. Even though each individual was having the same number of children, we still had differential reproduction — and that's how you had evolution. As long as there is a level of differential reproduction, we get evolutionary change. Even if it's not at the level of reproduction we necessarily think of.
