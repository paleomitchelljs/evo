A heterozygote is an individual with two different genetic ancestors for that locus. To have a heterozygote, there has to be more than one allele — if there's only one allele, a heterozygote is impossible.

You inherit alleles, not genotypes, and definitely not phenotypes. A genotype is a pairing. It is made fresh every generation out of whichever two copies happened to come together, and it is taken apart again when that individual breeds.

  • Four hundred individuals. One locus, two versions of the gene. Each individual is drawn as two halves, one per copy.
  • Nobody dies here and nobody is selected. The only control is who gets paired with whom.
  • At one end of the knob, partners are drawn from the whole pond at random. At the other, every partner is the closest relative there is.
  • The left bars are the three pairings you can count on the screen. The right bars are how many of each there would be if the pairing were at random.
  • The counter under the bars is the gap between them, as a fraction of the right-hand ones.

B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

You are literally zero genetically related to most of your ancestors. You get zero genes from a majority of them. Three generations back, you're usually still related to most. By eight generations back, someone has dropped out of your actual lineage. By around twelve generations back on average, you are genetically connected to fewer of your ancestors than the ones you're still connected to. They passed on zero of their genes that are still inside you.

You're not equally related to your grandparents. And you are not, on a genetic level, even at all related to some of your great-great-great-grandparents. You have no DNA in common with them. None of their DNA made it through to you. Maybe it did to your sister, or your cousin — they didn't just disappear from the family. But for any one individual, there's a set of ancestors in the past from whom you didn't actually inherit any DNA.

  • Nothing dies here and nothing is selected. The coin is the only thing happening.

Lots of people can genealogically chase themselves back to Genghis Khan. But not that many will actually have genes from Genghis Khan. And what genes different people have in common might differ. Genealogically, though, he'll persist for a very, very long time, because of how many descendants he has. Stage C is open.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Go 32 generations back — about 930 years, year 1100 AD — and you have more ancestors than there are base pairs in your DNA. About four billion. There are about eight billion people alive today. Were there more people alive in the year 1100? No. Something is wrong with this picture. The answer: we're all massively inbred. The genealogy doesn't bifurcate cleanly. The same individuals show up in your family tree multiple times.

There is no such thing as an unrelated person. The question is not, are they related — the question is to what extent.

As you inbreed, you have a more recent common ancestor. If I have a lot of inbreeding, that means my common ancestor is inherently more recent. Because that's what inbreeding is. It's having a more recent common ancestor with your mate than you'd expect by random chance. That's how we're going to define it: you and your mate are more closely related to one another than the population average would suggest.

  • A pond of birds, bred generation after generation. Every pairing is drawn and kept, so the whole pedigree is on the screen.
  • The knob is how partners are found. At one end, a partner is anyone in the pond. At the other, it is the closest relative available.
  • The highlighted pair has both its ancestries traced back, in two colours, and the first bird on both sides is ringed.
  • Two numbers are reported, and they are not the same number. The gap is counted off the two-tone birds exactly as in Stage A.
  • The other one is how much of the founders' variety is left, which is the thing Lesson 10 watched drain away.

Inbreeding speeds up drift because instead of one big population you've got smaller populations within which drift is faster. High inbreeding means your population isn't acting like one big one. The actual count is the same. Genetically, you're a bunch of small populations, not one big one. Because of inbreeding, we do not function like a population of 8 billion. Genetically we function like a population of 15,000. Vastly smaller. From the perspective of drift, that's the number that matters. Stage D is open.

Solve Stage C to unlock this section.

Recessive is not some extra property. Recessive is the word we use when something doesn't matter as a heterozygote. Things often don't matter as a heterozygote because they're broken, but there's a backup. You have about 100 mutations; almost all of them are either neutral, or they break something.

Take a hammer to a random spot on your car. That is what a mutation usually does. It is not that the hammer is aimed at anything. It is that there are far more ways to break a working thing than to improve one, and every one of us is already carrying the dents.

  • Two parents, and a hundred chicks from them. Every chick takes one copy of each gene from each parent.
  • Each founding genome carries some number of broken copies, scattered at random across the places in the genome. Nothing is wrong with a bird that carries one broken copy and one working one.
  • A chick that ends up with a broken copy at the same place from both parents is the one that does not hatch.
  • Three ways for the parents to be related, and two knobs: how many broken copies a founding genome carries, and how many places there are in the genome for them to sit in.
  • The strip under the chicks shows where the breakages are in each parent, and which places the two of them share.

In the general population, your chance of finding someone with the same constellation of broken alleles is roughly one over the mutation rate — very, very low. With a close relative, it's a lot higher, because you have a lot more in common that's identical-by-descent. Identical-by-descent of loss-of-function alleles is what drives the biological negative outcomes of incest. The mechanism is that close relatives share the same broken alleles inherited from the same recent ancestor, so their kids end up homozygous for the broken ones. Stage E is open.

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.

The most recent common ancestor is the actual real individual that connects two individuals. Every pair of individuals on the planet, at some point, came from a cell that divided in two. If you trace your cells back to your zygote, and that zygote back to your parents' zygotes, and so on — eventually you will find some cell, somewhere in the past, that divided and led down the line to you and to whoever else you're talking about.

The most recent common ancestor is not the first individual with a given allele. It's the most recent individual that all the living individuals carrying that allele can trace back to. The original allele arose earlier and got passed forward — but all our information about its early history is lost.

If I told you a car was driving at 50 mph and has gone 100 miles, how long ago did it leave? You have a distance and a speed — distance divided by speed gives you time. Number of mutations is your distance. The slope of mutations versus time is your speed.

  • One bird in a pond, and the two copies of one gene it is carrying.
  • Each copy came from one of its mother's two, or one of its father's two. Walk both of them back a generation at a time until they arrive at the same copy in the same bird.
  • Nothing here is selected, nobody dies out of turn, and the headcount never changes.
  • The second knob puts a mark on a copy whenever it is passed on, at whatever rate you set. Marks are how you can tell two copies apart.
  • A bird is two-tone at this gene when at least one mark sits on the path between its two copies.

Heterozygotes are a measure, in units of mutation rate, of how long ago your common ancestors for one locus were. And across your genome, that's the average for all of them. This is why heterozygosity works as a measure of drift, and so many other things. It is fundamentally a measurement of the rate of evolution — the rate of allele change — by looking at our ancestry. For any individual locus, the coalescent time is about four times the effective population size. That's the same number as fixation by drift. Because fixation and coalescence are the same process, run backwards.
