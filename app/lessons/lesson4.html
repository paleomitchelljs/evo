A — Wrong, but usefully wrong

Locked — confirm your name above to begin.

Models are wrong. But they can be usefully wrong. A model is a simplification of the world in order to better understand it. Fitting a model means finding the parameters that best predict the response from the predictors. The fit is how well that prediction matches reality. A model isn't true. It's just a useful tool to help you make sense of complicated data.

A graph can only tell you what's on the axes and in the legend. If it's not on the axes or in the legend, the graph can't tell you anything about it.

B — Look at sample sizes

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

When you read a developmental biology paper, look at sample sizes. You'll often see something like "4 out of 8 embryos showed this phenotype." That number is low enough that you should be skeptical. Skepticism shouldn't mean rejecting the paper. It should mean looking for follow-up experiments.

C — Sure about one, not sure about the other

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

The difference between a significant result and a non-significant result is itself not significant. If I test two drugs and one significantly improves survival and the other does not, that doesn't mean one drug is better. It means I'm sure one works, and I'm not sure about the other. Due to sample size, you can have a situation where the more effective drug has higher uncertainty and so isn't significant — and your best guess is that it is actually better than the significant one.
