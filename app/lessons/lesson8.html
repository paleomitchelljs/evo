B

Solve Stage A to unlock this section.

He literally invents linear regression to prove his idea of eugenics — that British people related to his cousin are better than British people who aren't related to his cousin. Which is the stupidest reason any statistical tool has ever been invented. Galton invents a whole branch of mathematics to try to say: if you know Darwin is smart, his cousin must also be smart. Which is not true.

These are his families. Every one of your five rules from Stage A is now one column of dots in here.

Grab the line and tilt it. The bar beside the plot is the children's whole variation, and the tilt decides how much of it the line reaches.

C

Solve Stage B to unlock this section.

Height isn't one gene. There are something like 1,700 individual loci associated with height, almost all of them very small effect — the largest single one we've ever found is associated with a 0.07-centimetre difference. Height is a thousand little things, and you add them up.

Below is a population built that way, and it can be taken apart, which Galton's families cannot. Two knobs: how big the pile a child is handed is, and how much a life adds on top of it.

Every stalk under the plot is now in two colours. Set the knobs so the tilt comes out where Galton's families put it, then start turning the life down.

  • 3,000 families. Two parents each, one grown child each, heights in inches.
  • A child is handed half of each parent's pile. Which half is a fresh draw every time.
  • The line is fitted for you here — Stage B was the fitting. This stage is about what it misses.
  • Each stalk runs from the line to the child in two colours: the part of the miss that is in what the child was handed, and the part a life added.
  • The bar is the same bar as Stage B, with the pale part split by those two colours.
  • The whole spread of the children moves when you move the knobs. Watch it, and watch what the tilt does and does not do.

The trait can vary, the trait can be genetic, but what matters is that the variation in the trait is genetic.

Solve Stage D to unlock this section.

Cut the tails off some puppies and only let the tailless puppies breed. That is selection — they make more of themselves, so puppies become more common. But taillessness doesn't become more common, because the trait isn't heritable. Selection is the differential reproduction step. Heritability is a separate step.

Here is the second step, priced. Drag the cut across the plot. Only the families to the right of it have children.

How far above the average you drag the parents is your choice. How far the children come is not.

  • 3,000 families again. The whole spread of the children is held at 2.55 inches here, whatever the knob is doing.
  • Drag the cut left or right. Families to the right of it breed; the rest are greyed out.
  • The upright line is where the chosen parents average. The flat line is where their children average.
  • Reported: how far the chosen parents sit above the average of everybody, and how far their children do.
  • The tilt does not move when you move the cut.
