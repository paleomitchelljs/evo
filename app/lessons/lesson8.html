Imagine you are curious about predicting whether some particular person has a trait. A genetic disease, say. Or a preference for a particular song.

You know that 72% of people whose parents have that trait also share it. 72% of people whose parents have the disease have the disease. 72% of people whose parents like the song like the song. So I tell you the parents have it. What is your prediction for the chance the child has it?

The answer is not as direct as it immediately seems. What we need to know is the chance the child would have that trait regardless of their parents. That is the comparison point. If 72% of all people have it, then knowing their parents adds no extra information, and the parents are not doing anything at all for our ability to predict. It can run the other way too. Say 90% of people like the song, and only 72% of the ones whose parents like it do. That is almost an anti-inheritance.

Heritability is our ability to predict a trait in an individual based on the state of the parent. That is all it is. The idea, and the way we measure it, were established long before we knew DNA was the basis of inheritance in a lot of biological systems. Roughly two of your lifetimes before. Most of how we measure it off a regression was worked out a full one of your lifetimes before we had the word gene.

A common problem students have is a misapprehension where causation is flipped. It is one thing to get correlation and causation flipped. It is another thing entirely to have the direction of causation flipped. Genes are heritable. Genetic differences are heritable. Heritability is not genetics.

To see that, take something purely genetic. The number of limbs a person has. It is determined genetically, and there is basically zero genetic variation in the population for it. There is still variation, though. People lose a limb from a fall, an accident, a predator, any number of ways, and none of those are genetic. Compare a parent's count to a child's and you get no correlation. They are completely separate.

Now add a heritability of danger. Risk proneness, risk-taking behavior, occupational hazards. Suddenly you can predict something like limblessness, which is not passed on genetically at all. If I tell you somebody's parent had black lung because they were a coal miner, the probability the child also has black lung is extremely high. Not because the parent passed on a genetic susceptibility. Because the probability somebody becomes a coal miner is a lot higher if their parent was one, and the probability someone gets black lung is a lot higher if they are a coal miner.

A lot of birds choose their mates based on the song they sing. A male will often sing a song that sounds like the song his father sang. A female will often prefer one that sounds like the song her father sang. The song is learned. So mating success and mate choice in adult birds is influenced strongly by the non-genetic nature of song.

Different birds have different strengths of preference and different degrees of similarity. In some birds the mothers sing as well as the fathers. In some, siblings come back and sing. There are many other routes by which a bird may learn a song. For this we are going to run a scenario where a bird models its song on its father and on nothing else.

You will run it twice. Once with the eggs left where they were laid, and once with the eggs swapped, so a chick is raised by a bird it did not come from. The question is the same both times. How much of the father's song ends up in the chick?

Francis Galton was Charles Darwin's cousin, and a horrible racist who ended up inventing the term eugenics. He wanted to prove, at least to himself, that being related to people of stature indicated something about an individual's quality. He spent a large fraction of his life trying to study the question of heritability.

He was working in 1885. There was absolutely no indication of how heritability worked, and in fact he did a lot of things incorrectly. It would be basically 50 years before DNA was nailed down as the molecule of inheritance. His work, despite its misguided factual basis and rather horrible motivations, was nonetheless very successful at its job of finding a way to measure heritability.

We are going to explore his data. 205 families who all had their heights measured, which lets us plot 934 grown children against their parents. Tilt a line onto them and lock it in. Then pick a set of parents, and put a bracket around the children they actually had.

  • Across: the two parents averaged, in inches. Up: the grown child, in inches.
  • Every female height is multiplied by 1.08, exactly as Galton did, so a father, a mother and a daughter sit on one scale.
  • The slider tilts the line. That is the deterministic part, the amount of a parent that arrives in a child.
  • The grey marks are the children's average in each strip of parents. A line that is tilted right runs through them.
  • Then pick a strip of parents. Their children light up; the rest of the plot goes grey.
  • Drag the bracket over those children — an edge to resize it, the middle to slide it. The page counts what it catches.
  • A prediction is a place and a width. The line gives you the place. The bracket is the width, and it is everything the line does not know.
  • Locking in the line reports two different numbers off it: the tilt, and the share of the children's variation the tilt accounts for.
