In 1775, a severe typhoon reduced the human population of Pingelap Atoll to roughly 20 survivors. By chance, one survivor (the king!) carried a recessive loss-of-function mutation for achromatopsia (complete colorblindness). Today, roughly 10% of the island’s population is completely colorblind, compared to 0.003% globally.

  1. What specific evolutionary mechanism caused this massive shift in allele frequency?
  2. Compared to the pre-typhoon population, how did this event alter the inbreeding coefficient (F) of the modern islanders?
  3. Before the typhoon why would this detrimental allele have existed in the population at all?
  4. Do you expect this allele to be at Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium?
  • Before the typhoon. The whole world, too large for chance to matter. Each generation the normal allele turns into the colorblind one with chance u. An individual's chance to have young: 1 with no colorblind copy, 1 − h·s with one, 1 − s with two. 20,000 generations.
  • The typhoon. 20 survivors, one of them carrying one colorblind copy. 100 islands, 9 generations (1775 to about 2000). The cost of colorblindness is the one you set before the typhoon.
  • Ten other genes, with many alleles each, ride along: how much of their variety is left measures F.
